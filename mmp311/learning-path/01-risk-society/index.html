Risk, uncertainty and the social production of relevance

MMP311 · Version 1.1 · Revised 19th of November, 2026 by Kristian Fuglseth (opens in a new tab)

Learning outcomes

  • Distinguish hazard, risk, uncertainty and ambiguity.
  • Explain manufactured, de-bounded and unequally distributed risk.
  • Use non-knowledge and unknown unknowns in a case analysis.
  • Identify who decides and who carries possible consequences.

Risk is not simply a measurable property of a hazard. It is also a way of anticipating possible harm under conditions of incomplete knowledge, disagreement and unequal exposure.

Hazard, risk, uncertainty and ambiguity

Hazard
A potential source of harm.
Risk
A possible development considered in relation to valued outcomes, decisions and consequences.
Uncertainty
Limits to what is known about probability, consequences, causal relations or future development.
Ambiguity
Disagreement about meaning, values, responsibility or acceptable action, even when actors share some of the same factual information.

Read for

  • Beck: de-bounding, anticipation and world risk society.
  • Sørensen: calculability, non-knowledge and constructivist realism.
Source basis. Sørensen shows that Beck’s risk concept is not equivalent to conventional calculable risk. Beck’s “new risks” combine manufactured origins with qualities associated with hazards: they may be difficult to calculate, insure, delimit and observe directly (Sørensen, 2018, pp. 6–12).

First and second modernity

In first modernity, industrial institutions treated uncertainty as something that could increasingly be measured, insured and controlled. Beck’s second or reflexive modernity describes a condition in which modernisation confronts the side effects of its own success. Scientific and technological development remains necessary, but also produces hazards, dependencies and non-knowledge that established institutions struggle to contain (Sørensen, 2018, pp. 7–9).

Manufactured and de-bounded risks

Beck’s world risk society is not defined by a simple increase in everyday danger. It is defined by risks that cross spatial, temporal and social boundaries. Beck uses ecological, financial and terrorist risks to show how consequences escape national control, unfold over long periods and make causal responsibility difficult to assign (Beck, 2002, pp. 40–45).

These risks are unequally distributed. Their transnational reach does not mean that all groups or regions are exposed in the same way. Decisions and benefits may be concentrated in one place while consequences are carried elsewhere (Beck, 2002, pp. 41–42; Sørensen, 2018, pp. 13–15).

Invisibility and non-knowledge

Many modern risks cannot be perceived directly. Radiation, climate change and toxic exposure become visible through scientific theories, instruments and institutional practices. Sørensen describes this through Beck’s idea of “second-hand non-experience”: citizens depend on expertise to identify risks while scientific disagreement can increase rather than remove uncertainty (Sørensen, 2018, pp. 7–10).

Beck distinguishes what is not yet known from “unknown unknowns”: knowledge whose absence is itself unknown when decisions are made. Risk communication must therefore explain both the evidence and the limits of present knowledge.

Organised irresponsibility and unequal consequences

When harm results from many lawful and distributed decisions, no single actor may appear responsible. Beck’s wider work describes this institutional diffusion as organised irresponsibility. The concept is analytically useful, but it should not be used as a substitute for tracing concrete authority, decisions, benefits and exposure in a particular case.

Worked example

CFC gases were introduced before their effect on the ozone layer was understood. The case shows how decisions can create consequences that were unknown at the time, and how scientific knowledge can later make an invisible risk actionable.

Student activity

Use a public risk assessment and three news reports about the same case. Identify the hazard, the assessed risks, explicit uncertainty, possible unknown unknowns and competing value judgements. Then state who makes decisions and who carries the possible consequences.

Common misunderstanding

Risk society does not mean that everyday life is simply becoming more dangerous. The concept concerns how modernisation produces risks that may be difficult to calculate, delimit, insure and observe directly.

Expected output

A six-row case matrix covering hazard, risk, uncertainty, ambiguity, decision-makers and exposed groups.

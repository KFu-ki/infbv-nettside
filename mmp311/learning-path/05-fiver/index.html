FIVER: from understanding to response

MMP311 · Version 1.1 · Last academically revised 19 November 2026 · Responsible academic: Kristian Fuglseth
Author-developed framework, first used in 2025. This English resource adapts Kristian Fuglseth’s Norwegian teaching text used with students at NTNU and HAW Kiel.

Learning outcomes

  • Use the five FIVER phases and fifteen work tasks.
  • Separate strategic aims from tactical tasks.
  • Identify organisational change before proposing communication measures.
  • Plan evaluation and institutional learning.

FIVER retains its Norwegian acronym: Forstå (frame and understand), Identifisere (identify), Vurdere (evaluate), Endre (change) and Respondere (respond). It is a five-stage framework for understanding and handling issues, possible problems and risks before they develop into crises. Trust-building, expectation management and learning remain strategic aims throughout the process.

1. Frame and understand

Map stakeholders and expectations, analyse drivers and the media environment, and establish structures for information and communication. The first stage should include communication, empathy, cooperation and cultural understanding, because these conditions affect whether the organisation is perceived as credible and responsible.

  1. Map stakeholders and their expectations.
  2. Analyse drivers, public debate and the media environment.
  3. Build structures and plans, using SOFT, FMECA, PSO or another suitable system.

2. Identify

Identify issues that may develop into problems. Clarify expectation gaps, distinguish relevant signals from noise, and prioritise matters according to their possible consequences and need for follow-up.

  1. Clarify expectation gaps.
  2. Distinguish noise from relevant risk signals.
  3. Prioritise and rank issues.

3. Evaluate

Assess probability, consequences, uncertainty and vulnerability. Locate causes and responsibility, examine gaps between stakeholder expectations and organisational practice, and test whether the matter has been framed too narrowly. The analysis must also establish whether the problem is communicative, organisational, financial, technical or operational.

  1. Analyse probability and consequence without treating the matrix as the whole assessment.
  2. Locate causes, responsibility and missing redundancy.
  3. Critically reassess the problem definition and examine expectation gaps. Reframing is not positive spin.

4. Change

Move from analysis to action. Choose a reactive, adaptive, proactive, initiative-taking or interactive strategy. Revise plans, resources, routines, roles and organisational structures. Distinguish material, organisational, strategic and communicative change. Adjusting the narrative cannot replace material or organisational change.

  1. Select a strategy suited to the issue and the organisation.
  2. Adapt plans, resources and measures.
  3. Revise communication, roles or structures where necessary.

5. Respond

Put decisions into practice. Communication must correspond with action, coordination must include relevant stakeholders and partners, and evaluation must occur during as well as after the response. Learning becomes institutional only when it changes plans, exercises, routines and culture.

  1. Act and communicate clearly.
  2. Coordinate and evaluate while the response develops.
  3. Institutionalise learning and improvement.

Worked example

A municipality receives repeated concerns about preparedness for a service interruption. FIVER starts by mapping stakeholders and expectations, not by drafting a message. The process must identify what plans, resources or roles should change before communication is selected.

Student activity

Use the fifteen tasks in a workshop. The group must identify at least one organisational change before proposing communication measures, and must state how learning will be assigned, documented and followed up.

Common misunderstanding

Reframing is not positive spin. It is a critical reassessment of the problem definition, relevant evidence, responsibility and possible response.

Expected output

A completed FIVER workshop record with evidence, decisions, responsibility, deadlines and evaluation criteria.

Using generative AI in this learning resource

Generative AI can support brainstorming, search planning, language revision, comparison and self-testing. It does not replace reading, source retrieval, disciplinary judgement or writing. Language models generate probable text and may present incorrect facts, references and interpretations. Verify every factual claim and reference in the original source.

  • Do not upload sensitive, confidential or copyrighted material to open AI services.
  • Check who provides the tool and what happens to submitted data.
  • Use Oria and academic databases for quality-assured literature searches; AI tools may supplement, but do not replace, a reproducible search.
  • Describe which AI tool you used, for what purpose, and what remained your own work.
  • Check the current rules for the course and institution before submitting assessed work.

Read the full Search & Write guidance on artificial intelligence.

Useful AI exercise: AI may help organise workshop notes or suggest missing questions in the five phases. It must not assign risk, responsibility or action without human review and traceable evidence.

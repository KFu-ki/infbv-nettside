Issues management in local government and the SOFT approach

MMP311 · Adapted teaching version · Revised 21st of November, 2026 by Kristian Fuglseth (opens in a new tab)

Municipal preparedness plans are designed to identify undesirable events, assess probability and consequences, and organise a response. This structure works well for many visible hazards. It is less effective when a crisis develops through weak communication, fragmented responsibility, contested interpretations or organisational misconduct. The SOFT approach was developed to bring these conditions into issue, risk and crisis analysis.

Basis for this teaching text. This page is an adapted English-language teaching version of the peer-reviewed book chapter “Issues Management in Local Government: A Case Study of Risk Assessment and Issue Management in National Contingency Plans for Local Governments” (Fuglseth, 2020). The chapter is on the MMP311 syllabus. The adaptation clarifies and develops the SOFT approach for use in the learning hub. It does not replace the published chapter.

The problem addressed by the chapter

Norwegian municipalities have a broad responsibility for the safety, health and welfare of their inhabitants. National regulations require them to identify undesirable events, assess their probability and consequences, and prepare plans for crisis management, warning, evacuation, resources and public information. These requirements establish a necessary structure. They do not, however, determine which situations the municipality is capable of recognising as risks.

The chapter examines a recurring imbalance in this work. Risk and preparedness planning has tended to emphasise visible and measurable events such as natural disasters, serious accidents, terrorism, ongoing violence and infrastructure failure. These events require preparation. The problem is that their prominence may narrow the field of attention. Human breakdowns, rumours, organisational misconduct, communication failures and conflicts over public perception may develop more gradually and remain outside the scenarios used in plans and exercises.

The distinction is not between “hard” events that matter and “soft” events that do not. A communication failure can alter an operational crisis. Fragmented responsibility can prevent early intervention. Rumours can redirect public attention and weaken the organisation’s ability to explain what is happening. Organisational misconduct can transform a manageable incident into a crisis of trust. These conditions have practical consequences even when they cannot be expressed through the conventional combination of probability and material loss.

Issues management before the crisis

Issues management adds an anticipatory perspective to risk and crisis management. The organisation does not wait for a predefined crisis threshold. It searches for emerging matters that may affect its objectives, stakeholders or legitimacy. This requires attention to the organisation’s surroundings as well as to internal routines, professional judgements and relations among services.

The chapter draws on the issues-management tradition developed by Chase (1984) and Heath (2013). In this tradition, issues management is not merely a method for defending reputation. It connects organisational decisions to public policy, stakeholder relationships and meaningful participation. The organisation must therefore be prepared to revise practices and policies, not only improve the presentation of decisions already made.

This point is particularly relevant to local government. Municipal administration must apply laws and general rules consistently. At the same time, local services encounter individual experiences, professional warnings and stakeholder concerns that do not always fit established categories. The administrative system may regard these observations as partial or subjective. Yet the observations may contain early signs of a problem that the formal risk assessment has not detected.

When coordination conceals responsibility

The chapter analyses a child-welfare case to examine how a crisis can develop even when several services and a formal coordination structure are available. Schools, health services, child-welfare services, specialist educational services and the police all had potential roles. The existence of an interdisciplinary system did not ensure that observations were combined, communicated or acted upon early enough.

The case illustrates a specific organisational risk. Coordination may strengthen the response when responsibilities, information and authority are clear. It may weaken the response when each service assumes that another part of the system is following the matter. A structure intended to reduce risk can then contribute to fragmented responsibility. The relevant analytical question is not only whether a coordination system exists, but how the system works in concrete cases.

The material does not establish that every serious outcome could have been predicted or prevented. It shows that formal structures alone do not document practical coordination. Early observations, professional judgement, communication across sectors and the ability to recognise a developing issue remain necessary.

What the crisis exercises revealed

The chapter also draws on crisis exercises and assessments involving municipalities and other local organisations. The exercises indicate that crisis-management teams had become more capable of handling accidents, evacuation, injuries and other familiar emergency scenarios. Difficulties became more visible when the exercises introduced uncertainty about internal routines, personnel conduct, rumours or responsibility.

In one exercise, a bus crash formed the initial operational scenario. The crisis-management team could apply established procedures to the accident. The situation changed when media players introduced information suggesting that defective brakes or inadequate internal controls might have contributed to the crash. The organisation then had to manage the accident, uncertain information, possible organisational responsibility and an emerging public frame at the same time.

The example demonstrates why communication cannot be treated as a final information task. Public questions may expose missing knowledge, conflicting interpretations or underlying organisational weaknesses. A prepared statement cannot resolve these conditions if the organisation lacks access to information, internal coordination or authority to change practice.

Exercises with predictable scenarios may also reproduce routine responses. Participants practise the procedures they already know, while the design leaves underlying vulnerabilities untouched. The value of an exercise therefore depends on which assumptions it tests, which disruptions it introduces and whether the evaluation examines organisational learning rather than procedural completion alone.

From crisis typologies to SOFT

The chapter compares municipal preparedness practice with crisis typologies developed by Lerbinger (1997), Coombs (2010), and Sellnow and Seeger (2013). Alongside natural disasters, technological failures and violent events, these typologies include malevolence, human breakdowns, rumours, public perception and organisational misconduct. The comparison reveals categories that receive less attention in conventional municipal scenarios.

SOFT was proposed as a procedure for directing attention towards these less visible conditions. Fuglseth (2020) organised the original SOFT procedure into four connected fields:

Table 1. The original SOFT procedure adapted from Fuglseth (2020, pp. 136–137).
Field Relevant skills and conditions Crisis typologies brought into view
S: Social Collaboration, communication, language and workplace culture Malevolence and human breakdowns
O: Opinion based Language and cognitive or emotional empathy Rumours
F: Framed Framing and perception Public perception
T: Transparent Personal habits, time management, teamwork and leadership Organisational misconduct

The table is a starting point rather than a complete causal model. Social conditions do not cause only one type of crisis, and rumours cannot be reduced to opinion. The categories direct attention towards relationships that a technical risk matrix can overlook. Their analytical value lies in the questions they generate and the evidence they require.

How the teaching approach has developed

The present MMP311 resource uses SOFT as a critical analytical lens. It retains the four original areas while making organisational conditions explicit. The development responds to a problem already visible in the chapter: skills do not operate independently of authority, resources, routines and institutional capacity.

The expanded teaching lens therefore examines five fields. Social conditions concern exposure, dependency, access and vulnerability. Opinions and expectations concern what stakeholders regard as legitimate, reasonable or safe. Framing concerns how causes, responsibilities, consequences and possible responses are defined. Transparency concerns what is known, unknown, withheld or difficult to explain. Organisational conditions concern authority, culture, resources, coordination and capacity for change.

This expansion is an adaptation of the original approach, not a claim that the peer-reviewed chapter presented an identical five-field model. The published chapter documents the basis for SOFT and its relation to overlooked crisis typologies. The learning resource develops the approach for analysis and teaching.

Using SOFT in analysis

SOFT supplements technical risk assessment. It does not replace assessment of probability, consequence, infrastructure, health, safety or operational capacity. The procedure is most useful after an initial situation analysis has been produced. The analyst then tests what the first account has excluded.

A practical analysis can proceed through five questions:

  1. Social conditions: Who is exposed, excluded or dependent, and what evidence documents these conditions?
  2. Opinions and expectations: Which expectations or values are in conflict, and whose statements support that interpretation?
  3. Framing: How are the problem, causes, responsibility and possible remedies defined?
  4. Transparency: What is known, uncertain, unavailable or difficult to communicate?
  5. Organisational conditions: Who has authority to act, which resources are available, and what prevents coordination or change?

The questions separate observation from interpretation. Stakeholder statements document articulated concerns, not the distribution of public opinion. Media coverage documents public representations, not organisational failure by itself. An exercise documents performance under the conditions created by the exercise, not how every real crisis will develop. Strong analysis depends on maintaining these distinctions.

Relationship to PRK and FIVER

SOFT performs a different task from PRK and FIVER. PRK examines conditional relationships among problems, risks and crises. It helps locate how one condition may produce or reshape another without assuming inevitable escalation. SOFT then challenges the situation analysis by testing which social, interpretive and organisational conditions remain outside it.

FIVER connects the revised interpretation to further work. Once missing conditions have been identified, the organisation must determine what requires investigation, validation, evaluation and response. SOFT widens the field of attention. It does not by itself decide which action is legitimate or effective.

Used together, the approaches prevent two reductions. Risk analysis is not reduced to public reaction, and communication is not reduced to message production. Analysis begins with documented conditions, examines how different actors interpret them and asks whether organisational practice must change.

Limits of the approach

Fuglseth (2020) presents a case-based and exploratory contribution. It combines literature, a local case and material from crisis exercises and interviews. The material supports an argument about blind spots in preparedness and the usefulness of a broader issues-management perspective. It does not provide a statistical test of the SOFT procedure across municipalities.

SOFT must therefore be used as an analytical and practical heuristic. Its categories help identify questions, evidence and possible omissions. Further empirical work is needed to establish how the approach performs across sectors, organisational forms and crisis types. The model is strongest when it makes assumptions open to examination rather than when its categories are treated as a checklist with predetermined answers.

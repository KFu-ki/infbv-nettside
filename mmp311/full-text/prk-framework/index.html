The PRK framework: Issues, problems, risks and crises

MMP311 · Adapted curriculum text · Kristian Fuglseth

The PRK framework connects issues management, risk communication and crisis communication. It helps analysts distinguish an emerging issue from a documented problem, specify the uncertainty that constitutes risk, and justify when a situation should be treated as a crisis. The categories are related, but they do not form an automatic or irreversible sequence.

Basis for the resource. This English curriculum text adapts the account of risk communication, issues management and crisis communication in Etterpåklok på forhånd, particularly pages 112–117 (Brataas et al., 2026). It also develops teaching by Kristian Fuglseth at Volda University College, NTNU and HAW Kiel, together with material from crisis exercises and municipal preparedness research.

1. Why PRK begins with an issue

Organisations rarely encounter a situation with an uncontested label. Management may see an isolated complaint. Employees may see a recurring organisational deficiency. Residents may see an unacceptable risk, while a regulator may see a question of compliance. Classification matters because it directs attention, responsibility and resources.

An issue arises when an actor makes a matter relevant by connecting an observation to an interest, value, expectation or possible consequence. The observation does not by itself prove that the organisation has failed. It establishes a reason to investigate. Issues management therefore begins with listening, evidence and competing problem definitions rather than with message production.

2. Problem, risk and crisis

Problem

A problem is a documented gap between an observed condition and a defensible obligation, objective, standard or expectation. Disagreement alone is not sufficient. The analysis must show what requires attention and why.

Risk

Risk concerns uncertainty about consequences for valued objectives. The assessment must identify possible outcomes, exposure and uncertainty, not merely public attention or organisational discomfort.

Crisis

A crisis threatens significant values and exceeds, or may soon exceed, ordinary handling capacity. Urgency, uncertainty, coordination needs and loss of control matter alongside material harm.

Brataas et al. (2026, pp. 112–113) distinguish preventive communication about hazards and uncertainty from communication in an acute crisis. Issues management occupies the field between them. It detects and handles matters that may reveal organisational problems, generate risk or constrain later crisis response (Brataas et al., 2026, p. 116).

PRK is not a ladder on which every issue climbs towards crisis. Most issues are clarified or handled. Some risks remain largely invisible. Some crises arise suddenly. Movement between categories must be demonstrated through evidence such as widening exposure, new harm, changed stakeholder behaviour, loss of ordinary control or formal crisis mobilisation.

3. Issue development and the tipping point

Gillions (2006, pp. 380–381) describes a six-phase sequence: initiation, interpretation, implication, ignition, influence and imposition. The model explains how technical or specialist debates can acquire a public and political agenda. The tipping point does not create the issue. It marks the stage at which an existing debate becomes intelligible and actionable for wider publics.

Three questions are especially useful near this point: Can a reasonable person identify who may be affected? Can the issue be visualised? Do the driving actors have sufficient credibility or organisational capacity to sustain attention (Gillions, 2006, p. 387)? These are questions about momentum, not tests of factual truth. A highly visible issue may rest on weak evidence, while a serious risk may remain difficult to communicate.

The tipping point and policy change

Gillions (2006, pp. 380–381) uses the tipping point to describe the moment when a debate moves from specialist interpretation into a wider public and political arena. After ignition, organised actors may seek to influence decision-makers. The final phase, imposition, concerns changes in regulation, legislation or other institutional conditions. The sequence is useful because it connects communication to changes in the environment in which organisations operate.

The model must nevertheless be used carefully. Public visibility does not prove that a particular issue caused subsequent policy change. The analyst must document the sequence, identify competing explanations and show which actors connected public mobilisation to an institutional decision. A regulatory outcome may reflect several cases, longer political processes, economic developments and legal obligations.

Jaques's non-linear relational model

Jaques (2007, pp. 147–149) argues that linear life-cycle models do not capture the full relationship between issues management and crisis management. Issues do not necessarily pass through fixed stages towards crisis. Organisations may work simultaneously with environmental scanning, issue identification, crisis prevention, operational response and post-crisis learning.

This correction is central to PRK. Gillions helps explain how a public issue may gain momentum and contribute to changed policy conditions. Jaques explains why that sequence must not be treated as an automatic law of escalation. PRK combines these insights by locating possible relationships among issues, problems, risks and crises while requiring evidence for every movement.

4. Haramsøya: a local issue becomes a national policy conflict

The essay uses Haramsøya as its principal empirical case because the conflict makes it possible to follow how a local and technically framed matter acquired actors, visual symbols, national attention and policy relevance. Other cases are developed on the PRK model page as comparative learning material rather than inserted as brief illustrations in the essay.

The Haramsøya wind-power conflict provides a detailed case for analysing issue development through PRK. A licence and an implementation process that developers and authorities understood primarily through legal and technical categories were interpreted locally through land use, landscape, property, procedural legitimacy and local self-government. The conflict cannot be reduced to a communication failure. It involved material interests, administrative authority, contractual obligations and competing values.

4.1 From licence to renewed controversy

The licence preceded the acute conflict by several years. When construction became imminent, residents encountered a project in a changed political and material context. The analytical question is therefore not only whether the licence remained legally valid. It is also whether the process retained legitimacy among actors who experienced new or altered consequences. Legal authority and social acceptance are related, but they are not identical.

4.2 The access road as a point of implication

The dispute over the access road gave the larger wind-power controversy a concrete location, visible action and identifiable actors. Hans Petter Thue and other local opponents contested the use of the road and physically obstructed construction traffic. Police intervention, vehicle removal and the movement of heavy machinery converted an abstract energy-policy conflict into a sequence that news media could document visually.

In Gillions's terms, the road dispute contributed to the implication and ignition phases. The conflict could now be narrated through affected residents, a disputed intervention and organisations associated with its implementation. This interpretation does not establish that one side's complete account was correct. It explains why the case acquired momentum.

4.3 Thue as an issue carrier

An issue carrier gives a dispersed conflict continuity, recognisable language and a point of contact for journalists and supporters. Thue's documented actions helped keep the road dispute and the wider development on the public agenda. The case became available for interpretation through values such as local autonomy, property rights, landscape protection and resistance to distant decision-making.

It would go beyond the evidence to claim that audiences supported Thue simply because they shared his values. A defensible formulation is narrower: the visible conflict made those values available as interpretive resources. Reception claims require audience data, interviews, comments or other evidence of how publics understood the events.

4.4 Expanding coalitions

Gillions (2006, pp. 382–384) distinguishes among commercial, governmental and not-for-profit coalitions. Haramsøya activated all three. Zephyr, Haram Kraft and Stangeland Maskin were connected to development and implementation. NVE, the ministry, the municipality, the police and the courts represented different forms of public authority. Local groups, Motvind Norge, environmental organisations, experts, journalists and political actors expanded the interpretive and organisational field.

The issue gained force as actors in different coalitions adopted overlapping definitions. They did not necessarily share all goals. Common cause around local participation, natural landscapes or criticism of wind-power governance was nevertheless sufficient to increase momentum.

4.5 Zephyr, NVE and Stangeland Maskin

The case illustrates how legal responsibility, operational responsibility and public attribution can diverge. NVE represented regulatory authority. Zephyr and Haram Kraft were associated with project development. Stangeland Maskin carried out contracted construction work and became a visible part of implementation. The contractor therefore faced reputational, operational and employee-related consequences without controlling the full licensing process.

PRK should map these different exposures rather than assign one undifferentiated crisis to every actor. A legitimacy problem for the licensing system, a contract problem for the entrepreneur, an operational delay for the developer and a political issue for elected representatives may interact while remaining analytically distinct.

4.6 From local visibility to policy influence

Haramsøya became part of a broader national conflict over land-based wind power, local participation and the legitimacy of the licensing regime. The case can therefore be analysed in relation to the influence phase. Yet causal claims require restraint. The available material supports the conclusion that Haramsøya contributed to national attention and mobilisation. It does not by itself prove that the case caused a particular election result, tax measure or legislative reform.

5. Communication as inquiry and intervention

Communication in PRK is not an ornamental layer added after management has decided what the situation means. It helps an organisation collect observations, test interpretations, identify affected publics and explain uncertainty. A listening process may show that the initial problem definition was incomplete. Internal communication may connect information that separate units cannot interpret alone.

Communication cannot substitute for material change. If an issue reveals unsafe practice, inadequate capacity or a rights violation, a clearer message does not resolve the problem. Proactive disclosure may reduce uncertainty, but credibility depends on whether the organisation also addresses the underlying condition (Brataas et al., 2026, pp. 116–117).

7. Relationship to SOFT and FIVER

PRK locates the analytical relationship among issues, problems, risks and crises. SOFT examines social conditions, expectations, framing, transparency and organisational capacity that the first situation analysis may overlook. FIVER organises the movement from understanding and identification to evaluation, change and response.

The approaches are complementary. PRK asks what kind of condition the organisation is facing and how it may change. SOFT asks which assumptions and actors have been excluded. FIVER asks how analysis becomes accountable action. None replaces sector expertise, legal assessment or technical risk analysis.

8. Methodological limits

PRK is an analytical and pedagogical framework. It is not a validated numerical scale, a universal threshold for declaring a crisis or a causal law of escalation. Researchers must operationalise each category and match claims to the material. Texts document articulated definitions and frames. Interviews document experiences and interpretations. Operational records document decisions and capacity. Media visibility documents attention, not necessarily material severity or audience agreement.

The framework is most useful when it slows premature classification. It requires the analyst to identify the issue, document the problem, specify the risk and justify any crisis designation. The result is not certainty. It is a more transparent basis for judgement and intervention.

Media distortion, news framing and mediatization

MMP311 · Version 1.0 · Revised 19th of November, 2026 by Kristian Fuglseth (opens in a new tab)

Issue, risk and crisis analysis often begins before a situation has acquired a stable definition. An event, complaint, policy proposal or public controversy may constitute an issue for one actor, a problem for another and a possible risk for a third. Analysis must therefore examine how the situation becomes relevant, which consequences are made visible, which values organise disagreement and which actors acquire the authority to define what the situation concerns.

News media constitute one part of the environment under analysis. Their significance does not lie only in disseminating information. Journalistic selection and presentation affect which aspects of a situation become publicly visible, how actors and consequences are connected, and which definitions appear credible or actionable. Public communication may therefore influence the conditions under which organisations detect issues, assess problems and risks, and decide whether a response requires communication, organisational change or both.

Media distortion, news framing and mediatization describe different levels of this process. Media distortion identifies techniques used to select and present information. Framing concerns how selected elements are organised into interpretations of problems, causes, responsibilities and possible responses. Mediatization concerns longer-term changes in the relationships between media and other institutions. Maintaining these distinctions makes it possible to move from close analysis of a particular text to an assessment of public relevance and, where the material permits it, institutional change.

The original concept of media distortion

Hernes first developed the concept of medievri in the 1970s. The original argument addressed the transition from an information-poor to an information-rich society. The institutional question was how media altered the internal functioning of other social units and the relationships among them (Hernes, 1977, p. 181). In the information-rich society, abundance created a shortage of attention and assessment time. Selection and presentation consequently became central.

The original model identified sharpening, simplification, polarisation, intensification and concretisation. Sharpening makes a principal point more distinct. Simplification reduces complexity. Polarisation organises actors and positions through contrast. Intensification strengthens conflict or confrontation. Concretisation gives priority to observable events and examples over abstract relationships (Hernes, 1977, pp. 187–188). Personification was omitted from the original article and added later (Hernes, 2019, p. 30).

These techniques do not necessarily indicate factual error, deliberate manipulation or poor journalism. Hernes tied them to limited capacity, assumptions about normal knowledge, democratic accessibility and the need to attract an audience (Hernes, 1977, p. 188). The analytical task is therefore to show what each operation does in the material. Sharpening may clarify an issue while narrowing the range of relevant interpretations. Concretisation may make an abstract risk understandable through a particular case while obscuring structural conditions. Personification may make responsibility visible while directing attention away from organisational routines or institutional relationships.

The techniques are methodologically relevant in analysis work in issue and risk management because they help separate the underlying matter from its mediated presentation. They provide concrete questions for close reading. What has been sharpened? What complexity has been removed? Which actors or positions are organised as opposites? Which example is made representative? Which institutional process is presented through an individual?

Media distortion 2.0

Hernes (2019) revisits the original model from a media environment that differs structurally from the system in which it was developed. The 2019 article first reconstructs the transition to information abundance and restates the original techniques (Hernes, 2019, p. 14). It then traces the fragmentation of the newspaper-industrial complex, digital convergence, continuous news flows, personalised distribution and the movement from one-to-many communication towards interactive all-to-all communication (Hernes, 2019, pp. 21–27).

The revision is therefore more extensive than an update to a list of presentational techniques. Professional news organisations no longer exercise the same degree of control over access to mass publicity. Digital platforms allow political actors, organisations and users to publish, distribute and amplify material directly. Selection still occurs, but it is distributed across editorial routines, platform structures, strategic actors and audience activity.

Hernes develops the argument through conflict and emotionalisation. Information enters an existing field of tensions rather than a social vacuum, and new issues can reactivate established front lines and group identities (Hernes, 2019, p. 28). Circulation is not neutral distribution. People can mobilise, warn, endorse and oppose in real time. Hernes captures this affective potential in the formulation “When we care, we share” (Hernes, 2019, p. 28).

The next step concerns conflict organisation. A new matter can function as a loyalty test and activate latent conflicts. How the conflict is defined affects who becomes involved, what the dispute concerns and how strongly the parties mobilise (Hernes, 2019, p. 29). A single event may become a multi-issue that divides opinion in several ways and mobilises different publics for different reasons. Hernes’s conflict thesis is that an information-rich society contains not only a struggle for attention, but also competition over issues capable of activating latent conflicts (Hernes, 2019, p. 29).

Media distortion 2.0 thus extends the original model from journalistic presentation towards the broader organisation of attention, circulation and conflict. The original techniques remain relevant, but they operate in a changed infrastructure. Sharpening and polarisation can be performed by news organisations, political actors, organisations and users. Intensification may occur through repeated circulation, commentary and platform interaction. Personification may connect a structural issue to one individual whose actions become a focal point for wider conflicts.

This development is directly relevant to environmental assessment. Organisations may first encounter an emerging issue through fragmented signals distributed across news media, social platforms, stakeholder communication and political debate. High visibility does not in itself document material severity. Extensive circulation may reflect emotional mobilisation, strategic amplification or the activation of an established conflict. Conversely, an issue with limited media visibility may still represent a substantial organisational problem or risk.

News framing and the construction of meaning

Entman (1993) places selection within a wider process of meaning-making. Framing involves selecting aspects of perceived reality and making them more salient in communication. A frame may support a problem definition, a causal interpretation, a moral evaluation and a proposed response (Entman, 1993, p. 52). Framing is consequently more than choosing a topic or angle. It concerns how several elements are organised into an interpretation of what has happened, why the situation matters and what should follow.

A frame cannot be established from one word, source or isolated feature. Beyer (2010) describes frames as constructions that emphasise certain aspects while connecting the selection to definitions, causes, consequences and evaluations (Beyer, 2010, p. 163). The analytical object is the relationship among the elements. A reference to disagreement does not by itself establish a conflict frame. The analysis must show how disagreement structures the presentation and connects actors, causes, values and possible outcomes.

Framing overlaps with agenda-setting without becoming identical to it. First-level agenda-setting concerns the relative prominence of issues. Second-level agenda-setting concerns the prominence of attributes connected to issues or actors. Framing places greater emphasis on relationships among elements and on the construction of causes, evaluations and meaning. Beyer (2010) places framing, agenda-setting and priming in the same theoretical discussion without subsuming them under one conceptual framework (Beyer, 2010, p. 164).

The distinction clarifies the role of news values in environmental assessment. Timeliness, conflict, proximity, consequence, personalisation and continuity may help explain why a matter attracts journalistic attention. These criteria do not document stakeholder attitudes or public opinion. Nor do they determine whether the matter constitutes an issue, problem or risk. They provide indicators of possible public relevance. Framing analysis then examines how selected elements organise this relevance into a particular account.

Frames in the communication process

Beyer (2010) distinguishes strategic frames, journalistic frames, news or media frames, and framing effects (Beyer, 2010, p. 165). Strategic frames are promoted by actors who seek to define a matter before or during journalistic processing. Journalistic frames guide selection and construction in news work. News frames are observable in published content. Framing effects concern the encounter between the mediated account and its audiences.

The distinction defines what different materials can document. A content analysis can identify patterns in published news but cannot by itself establish a journalist’s intention. A news report containing an organisation’s definition does not prove that the organisation successfully controlled the frame. A textual frame does not document how a public interpreted the matter.

Beyer (2010) associates interviews, observation and document analysis with the study of strategic and journalistic framing, while frames in published content can be examined through content analysis (Beyer, 2010, p. 165). Audience responses require reception-oriented material. Framing research may investigate cognitive, attitudinal and behavioural responses, but these effects cannot be inferred from the media text alone (Beyer, 2010, p. 166).

Knudsen (2016) expresses the same methodological separation by treating news frames as both dependent and independent variables. As a dependent variable, a frame is something to be explained through actors, routines, events and institutional conditions. As an independent variable, it is examined as a possible influence on how audiences, organisations or decision-makers interpret an issue (Knudsen, 2016, p. 10).

Environmental assessment draws on forms of material that must not be treated as equivalent. Media texts document public representations. Stakeholder documents and statements document articulated positions. Interviews may provide access to experiences and interpretations. Surveys can document distributions of attitudes within a defined population. Organisational records may document decisions and changes in practice. Each source supports different conclusions.

Generic frames and methodological precision

Beyer (2010) distinguishes issue-specific frames from generic frames. Issue-specific frames organise the interpretation of a particular matter. Generic frames may occur across policy areas, periods and media systems. Conflict, personalisation, human interest, responsibility and game or strategy are examples of generic structures. The distinction affects research design because issue-specific frames are often developed inductively, whereas generic frames are commonly examined deductively (Beyer, 2010, p. 165).

Coding only the dominant frame creates a problem. A news report may contain conflict, substantive policy discussion, personalisation and strategic interpretation in different degrees. Requiring the analyst to select one dominant category removes information about how the frames coexist. Beyer argues that broad, holistic categories also weaken conceptual validity because they do not show which features led the coder to identify the frame (Beyer, 2010, p. 167).

Each generic frame should therefore be operationalised through several concrete questions. In an analysis of conflict framing, one variable may assess whether the report presents disagreement among parties, individuals, groups or countries. The variables connect an abstract concept to observable textual features (Beyer, 2010, p. 168).

The variables may be coded through graded scales rather than a binary distinction between presence and absence. A graded scale can distinguish among no presence, limited presence, substantial presence and principal focus. This procedure retains more information about partly latent textual properties (Beyer, 2010, p. 169). It also permits several frames to occur in the same unit. Beyer’s empirical example shows that game, issue, personalisation and conflict frames can coexist and correlate in different ways (Beyer, 2010, p. 174).

These principles remain relevant in qualitative analysis. A frame should be reconstructed from several observations. The analyst should state which features support the interpretation, consider competing frames and avoid presenting latent meaning as self-evident. If systematic coding is used, the scale, codebook and reliability procedure must be documented.

From framing to mediatization

Hjarvard (2016) defines mediatization as a process in which relationships between media and other social and cultural domains change and create altered conditions for communication and interaction (Hjarvard, 2016, p. 39). The concept moves the analysis from particular messages to institutional relationships and change over time.

Mediation and mediatization must remain distinct. Mediation concerns communication through a medium. Mediatization concerns longer-term changes in communicative conditions and institutional practices. A political statement on a digital platform is an instance of mediation. Evidence that political organisations have changed routines, staffing, timing or decision-making in anticipation of media formats may support a mediatization argument.

Knudsen (2016) connects framing to this institutional level. Frames can be studied both as outcomes of journalistic, strategic and institutional conditions and as possible influences on publics, decision-makers and organisations (Knudsen, 2016, p. 10). The relationship is not one-directional. Political and organisational actors promote frames, manage access to information and attempt to influence journalistic definitions. News organisations select, modify or contest these strategic frames (Knudsen, 2016, p. 12).

Media logic provides a possible meeting point. Frames belong to its aesthetic dimension because they concern recurrent forms of presentation, dramaturgy and emphasis. They also acquire an institutional dimension when stable relations among media organisations, professional routines and preferred sources reproduce characteristic accounts of issues and actors (Knudsen, 2016, p. 12).

The development from the original medievri to media distortion 2.0 makes this institutional connection more visible. The original model identifies techniques through which editorial media format information. The revised model addresses an environment where these operations are distributed among more actors and infrastructures. Framing explains how selected elements form interpretations. Mediatization asks whether recurring conditions of selection, circulation and framing contribute to changes in organisational practice.

The concepts do not form a deterministic sequence. A presentational technique does not automatically produce one frame. A frame does not guarantee an audience response. Repeated circulation does not by itself establish institutional change. Knudsen concludes that framing research alone may fail to explain how media influence reaches the institutional level, while mediatization research alone may fail to identify the communicative mechanisms through which influence develops (Knudsen, 2016, p. 16).

The integrated contribution to analysis work in issue and risk management

The theoretical development supports a layered approach to environmental assessment. The first layer concerns the underlying matter. What has occurred? Which evidence is available? What remains uncertain? Which possible consequences follow from the documented conditions?

The second layer concerns relevance. Who has made the matter visible? Which publics recognise a consequence or shared concern? Which values organise their interpretations? News values may indicate why the matter attracts attention, but they do not measure the seriousness of the issue or the distribution of public attitudes.

The third layer concerns presentation and framing. Which elements have been selected? Which techniques shape the presentation? How are the problem, causes, responsibilities and possible responses organised? Which interpretations are made prominent, and which alternatives disappear from view?

The fourth layer concerns circulation and conflict organisation. Does the matter activate established oppositions? Does emotional mobilisation amplify the issue? Does the same event function as a multi-issue for publics organised around different values?

The fifth layer concerns organisational interpretation. Has the organisation treated visibility as evidence of material risk? Has it recognised the relevant publics? Does its first analysis reproduce the dominant frame? Which additional evidence is needed before the matter can be categorised as an organisational problem or risk?

The sixth layer concerns institutional adaptation. Has the organisation changed routines or priorities because it anticipates recurring media formats and expectations? Is the observed change temporary and case-specific, or does it indicate a longer-term alteration in communicative conditions?

PRK, SOFT and FIVER organise different parts of this work. PRK provides categories for examining how a matter is defined and how it may move conditionally among issue, problem, risk and crisis. SOFT tests the social and organisational assumptions underlying the situation analysis. FIVER connects interpretation to evaluation, organisational change and response. Media distortion, framing and mediatization do not replace these models. They provide theory and method for analysing the mediated environment in which the models are applied.

This integration preserves a necessary boundary. Media analysis can identify representations, visibility and framing. Stakeholder analysis can document articulated interests and positions. Reception material can document interpretations and attitudes. Risk assessment can evaluate possible outcomes and uncertainty. Organisational analysis can document decisions and institutional change. Strong conclusions depend on maintaining these distinctions rather than allowing one kind of material to stand in for another.

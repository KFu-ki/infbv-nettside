Analytical framework · PRK

Problem, risk and crisis

PRK follows how contested problems, risk assessments and crisis conditions can develop into one another. The movement is circular rather than inevitable or linear.

Use PRK in a caseCompare the models

What PRK helps you see

Problem

An organisational problem becomes visible when conduct, expectations or interpretations no longer align. Different stakeholders may disagree about both the problem and the appropriate response.

Risk

The issue represents risk when possible developments threaten people, values, operations, legitimacy or relationships. Assessment must include uncertainty and vulnerability, not only probability.

Crisis

A crisis places operations, decision-making or legitimacy under acute pressure. Crisis response can also expose older problems and produce new expectations.

Use PRK in a case

  1. 01

    Describe the matter

    What has happened, who is affected, and which expectations appear to be broken?

  2. 02

    Locate the problem

    Is the core problem operational, organisational, communicative, political, or a combination?

  3. 03

    Assess development

    Which values are at stake, what is uncertain, and what could make the situation more serious?

  4. 04

    Consider crisis pressure

    Could established capacity, authority, trust or coordination become insufficient?

  5. 05

    Feed learning back

    What must change in decisions, resources, routines or preparedness?

Working strategically and tactically with PRK

PRK separates strategic analysis from tactical communication while connecting problems, risks and crises across time. Before a crisis, organisations must work with emerging problems and possible risks. During a crisis, they must manage the underlying problem, changing risks and acute crisis communication at the same time. After the acute phase, unresolved problems may create new risks and secondary crises.

Strategic level: Problem

Organisations must continually scan their surroundings for matters that may become contested. The first task is to establish whether anyone experiences the matter as a problem. This provides the basis for identifying stakeholders, expectations and competing interpretations. Only then can the organisation assess whether the matter represents a risk and what forms of intervention may be required.

Strategic work requires openness to weak signals and perspectives outside the organisation. No organisation operates in a vacuum. Unknown or neglected factors can become visible during an acute event and may develop into a secondary crisis.

Strategic level: Risk

When a contested matter may threaten people, services, values, legitimacy or relationships, it must enter the organisation's wider risk assessment. Analysis should examine stakeholders, possible consequences, uncertainty and vulnerability. Relevant scenarios should inform preparedness exercises, priorities and risk communication.

Strategic level: Crisis

During a crisis, the strategic objective is to limit harm and consequences. Communication is more than message distribution. It is an exchange of knowledge intended to reduce uncertainty, support coordinated action and limit secondary harm.

Crisis managers must continue to identify problems that could generate new crises. The triggering event is not always what causes the most serious long-term consequences. Organisational conduct, unmet expectations or poor coordination can prolong and intensify the crisis.

The PRK framework is based on teaching developed by Kristian Fuglseth at Volda University College, NTNU and HAW Kiel, and on experience from crisis exercises and municipal preparedness research.

Cases and evidence

Use PRK to analyse issue development

PRK does not assume that every issue becomes a crisis. The expanded material asks students to document when a matter becomes a problem, what uncertainty constitutes risk and what evidence would justify crisis mobilisation.

Haramsøya and the tipping point

Analyse how an access-road dispute, local mobilisation, police intervention and visual news coverage connected a local wind-power conflict to national policy debate.

Fosen and rights-based reframing

Compare a technical and administrative account of renewable-energy development with a definition grounded in Indigenous cultural rights.

Contractor exposure

Distinguish the responsibilities and risks of NVE, Zephyr, Haram Kraft and Stangeland Maskin. Explain why public attribution may differ from legal responsibility.

Evidence task

Build a timeline and identify a candidate tipping point. Separate evidence of media attention from evidence of changed policy, behaviour or operational capacity.

Comparative case library

Ethics, rights and broken expectations

These cases extend PRK beyond crisis labels. Each case asks how a public expectation is formed, what makes a breach visible, who carries the issue into a wider arena, and when ethical claims become organisational duties, legal rights or regulatory change. Open a case to examine the concepts, evidence and questions.

Ella Marie Hætta Isaksen: Indigenous rights, Fosen and issue reframing

The Fosen conflict shows how the definition of a situation determines what counts as an adequate response. A technical frame emphasises renewable electricity and project operation. A rights frame asks whether state action protects Sámi cultural practice after the Supreme Court judgment.

Isaksen's public role did not create the underlying right. Cultural expression, public argument and non-violent protest helped sustain a definition grounded in Indigenous rights and state responsibility. Fritt Ord describes the mobilisation as connecting culture, activism and minority protection, and presents Isaksen as a prominent participant in the peaceful Fosen demonstrations.

PRK questions

  • Which expectation was broken: consultation, compliance with the judgment, cultural protection, or all three?
  • When does delayed implementation become a problem, a risk to legitimacy, or a constitutional crisis?
  • How did cultural symbols and civil disobedience change the visibility of the issue?

Erin Brockovich: lived experience, water and corporate accountability

Brockovich provides a case for analysing how individual observations become a documented public issue. Reports of illness or altered water quality do not by themselves establish causation. They can nevertheless identify a pattern that requires records, sampling, legal investigation and comparison with company accounts.

The ethical issue concerns the distribution of knowledge and burden. Residents may be expected to prove harm after exposure, while an organisation controls technical information and resources. PRK asks when that asymmetry becomes an organisational problem and which uncertainties constitute risk.

PRK questions

  • What turns an individual complaint into evidence of a shared condition?
  • Who controls the data needed to document exposure and consequence?
  • When should precaution replace demands for complete causal certainty?

Gro Harlem Brundtland: future generations and sustainable development

The Brundtland formulation connects present needs to the ability of future generations to meet their needs. In PRK terms, it expands the affected public beyond actors who can speak in the present. A decision may therefore create risk even when immediate users experience a benefit.

Wind power and data centres make the distributive question concrete. Who receives electricity, investment or digital capacity? Who carries land-use, water, grid and ecological costs? Sustainable-development language does not answer these questions unless the analysis identifies actors, time horizons and thresholds.

PRK questions

  • Which benefits and burdens occur now, and which are deferred?
  • Whose needs define the project's public value?
  • What evidence would show that a sustainability promise has become an expectation gap?

Martha Nussbaum: capabilities, dignity and environmental justice

The capabilities approach asks what people are actually able to do and to be. Aggregate benefit is insufficient if a project pushes particular groups below defensible thresholds for health, cultural practice, political participation or control over their material environment.

This approach clarifies broken expectations in PRK. The relevant question is not only whether an organisation fulfilled a formal procedure. The analyst must also examine whether affected groups retained real opportunities to participate, maintain health, practise culture and influence the conditions that shape their lives.

PRK questions

  • Which capabilities are affected by land, water or energy decisions?
  • Can compensation restore the specific capability that is lost?
  • When does unequal exposure become an issue of justice rather than ordinary stakeholder disagreement?
